Challenge

Its 880K followers were slipping, engagement was dropping, and without a clear editorial system, the feed felt scattered.

Solution
I rebuilt @milk’s editorial strategy around defined content pillars and ran it for a year, leading everything from creative outreach and content production to editorial planning and posting.

The Refillery

Behavior-First Strategy That Turns Refill Into Ritual

Challenge
Beauty produces over 120 billion units of packaging a year, and most of it is too small or too mixed in material to recycle. Curbside systems confuse consumers, and the programs retailers run to address it go unpromoted, digitally and physically. Distinctive packaging is core to how beauty brands stand out in a sea of products. So the real question became: how do you make reuse the easy choice without asking brands to redesign?
Approach

I interviewed three people across the system: a micro beauty influencer, a PACT Collective program manager, and a Sephora sales associate. I paired those conversations with a digital audit of beauty retailer websites. Three insights shaped the direction:

  • Convenience beats conscience. Consumers feel guilty tossing empties but do it anyway, because in-store drop-offs ask them to go out of their way.
  • The system is the barrier. Recycling programs exist, but staff aren't trained to mention them and retailer sites bury them in footers. Users who do find the bins often contaminate them.
  • Adoption is social. People join when their peers do. Refill culture was already growing on TikTok, which made it the natural place to normalize the behavior.

From there, I built two personas, a sustainably curious early adopter and a trend-driven early majority shopper, and mapped them onto the diffusion of innovation curve to plan who The Refillery would reach first and how.

1 / 6
Solution
The Refillery is a return-clean-refill system that works with the packaging beauty brands already make. Users drop off empties at a Refillery location or schedule a pickup through the app. On-site machinery cleans and refills each container before it heads back to its owner.

An app that removes the effort. Pickup and delivery bring reuse to the doorstep, so convenience works for reuse instead of against it. A tracking dashboard turns every return into visible progress (“You’ve saved 12 bottles”), and short videos of the cleaning process answer hygiene concerns up front.

A rollout that makes refill social. Early adopters lead, and creators carry the behavior to the mainstream. Partner creators get free trials to feature refilled products in GRWMs and before-and-afters, building on refill content that’s already trending on TikTok.

Outcome

The final deliverable was a full service concept: a stakeholder map, user personas, an adoption strategy grounded in diffusion of innovation, app screens, and a launch campaign spanning TikTok and out-of-home.

The concept resonated across the system. The PACT Collective program manager saw it as more intuitive than current recycling infrastructure and likely to reduce user error, and the influencer I interviewed said app-based pickup would feel as natural as ordering on Grab.

What I’d test next: whether refilling can match or beat the price of buying new, the biggest hesitation in my interviews, and how much proof of impact a retail partner like Sephora would need before signing on.

This project taught me that sustainability is a communication problem as much as a design one.

PepsiCo x Yum! Brands

Designing a Reusable Cup Pilot for Yum! Brands

Challenge
How can PepsiCo Beverages North America create a reusable cup ecosystem tailored for fast food chains under Yum! Brands to deliver value for PepsiCo, these restaurants (customers), and consumers?
Approach

We started by auditing PepsiCo’s value chain and found its sustainability efforts concentrated upstream in farming, manufacturing, and water. Then we studied existing reuse pilots and mapped every stakeholder the system would touch in Kumu.

  • Sustainability stops at the counter. PepsiCo’s biggest gap was consumer engagement. The moment a customer finishes a drink, the system loses them.
  • Free cups don’t come back. In Petaluma’s citywide trial, return rates dropped when cups were free. A deposit gives customers a reason to return the cup.
  • Loyalty already exists. Taco Bell’s systems already run tiered rewards and challenges. Building on it meant customers wouldn’t need to adopt anything new.
Solution
A gamified reuse system shared across Yum! Brands where customers borrow a cup for a $2 deposit, return it to any Yum! location, and earn points toward rewards. PepsiCo cuts single-use waste, restaurants build repeat visits, and consumers get rewarded for reusing.
Outcome

We delivered a pilot-ready concept for a systems and stakeholders map, the cup and rewards design, smart bin infrastructure, and a three-month rollout plan across Yum! Brands locations in Midtown and near NYU.

We designed around the risks as carefully as the rewards. Collectible cups can encourage overconsumption, so each customer can unlock the PATH bottle only once. Hygiene concerns are handled by back-of-house washing on a fixed cleaning protocol, and smart bins take the return process off staff.

What we’d measure: weekly return rates, cups lost, and staff and customer feedback, to test whether a $2 deposit and a points system can make returning the cup a habit.

This project showed me that the most sustainable system is the one that feels like a reward, not a chore.
